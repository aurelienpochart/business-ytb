## 0. Hook

> Logo Boeing (usage informatif) sur une photo d'usine. Compteur « 806 airplanes delivered, 2018 ».

In 2018, Boeing delivered eight hundred and six airplanes, more than any other company on earth. [B1] That year, it kept ten and a half billion dollars as profit. [B2]

> Barres annuelles du résultat net : 2018 en or, puis six années en rouge brique qui plongent. Cumul « −$35.7B ».

Then the numbers turned. From 2019 to 2024, Boeing lost almost thirty-six billion dollars. [B2] [B4] In the same years, two crashes killed three hundred and forty-six people, [B18] a panel blew out of a brand-new jet in mid-air, [B22] and a strike shut its biggest factories for almost two months. [B29]

> Énoncé : « 1997 ». Puis photo d'un 747 ou d'un 707 historique.

Here's a strange detail. When Boeing posted its loss for 2019, it was the company's first losing year since 1997. [B3] That is the year Boeing bought its rival, McDonnell Douglas. [B6]

For most of a century, Boeing was the engineers' company. So how did it end up here?

Not with one bad airplane. With a slow change in what the company was trying to get right.

Let's go back to when Boeing was run by people who built airplanes.

> Ident The Cost Curve, 3 s.

## 1. Bet the company

> Chronologie animée qui démarre : 1954 Dash 80, 1969 747, 1994 777. Photos d'archives.

In 1954, a prototype called the Dash 80 took off from Boeing Field in Seattle. It became the Boeing 707. [B10]

Nobody had ordered it. Boeing paid for it with its own money, about sixteen million dollars. One account says the boss persuaded the board to "bet every penny in Boeing's coffers". [B10] It worked. The 707 launched the jet age.

> Photo : le premier 747 en vol, 1969.

Fifteen years later, Boeing did it again. In February 1969, the first 747 took off from Everett, north of Seattle. [B11] The first jumbo jet.

> Photo : 777, premier vol 1994. Tuiles : « 100% digital design », « 8 airlines at the table ».

And in the early nineties came the 777, the first jetliner designed entirely on computers, with eight airlines sitting in on the design. Boeing called the group "Working Together". [B12]

> Énoncé : « Engineers first. »

The pattern was always the same. Spend a fortune up front, get the engineering right, and the money follows for decades.

By the mid-nineties, a third planemaker was running out of road.

## 2. The merger

> Logo McDonnell Douglas. Chronologie : décembre 1996, annonce ; 1er août 1997, fusion.

McDonnell Douglas, based in St. Louis, built fighter jets and airliners like the MD-11. But by 1996, it won only about five percent of new airliner orders. Boeing had around sixty percent, Airbus about thirty-five. [B7]

> Donut : parts de commandes 1996.

In December 1996, Boeing agreed to buy it in a stock deal worth thirteen point three billion dollars. The merger closed on August first, 1997. [B6]

Boeing was the buyer. But many of the people who ended up running it came from McDonnell Douglas, above all Harry Stonecipher, who became president, and later chief executive. [B7]

> Citation de Stonecipher, 1998.

In 1998, Stonecipher told employees to "quit behaving like a family and become more like a team". [B7]

> Citation de Stonecipher, 2004 (Chicago Tribune).

Years later, in 2004, he said it even more clearly: "When people say I changed the culture of Boeing, that was the intent, so that it's run like a business rather than a great engineering firm." [B7]

> Carte : Seattle et Chicago, une ligne de 2 800 km entre l'usine et le siège.

In 2001, Boeing moved its headquarters from Seattle to Chicago, helped by about sixty million dollars of tax credits. [B8] Its top executives now sat about seventeen hundred miles from the factories.

> Énoncé : « What gets measured: the airplane, or the quarter? »

None of this was unusual. Plenty of companies want to be run like a business. But in aviation, the engineering is the business.

## 3. The Dreamliner

> Photo : 787 Dreamliner. Nouveau visuel : silhouette d'avion vue de dessus, sections qui s'allument une à une avec le pays du partenaire.

The test was the 787 Dreamliner, a long-range jet made largely of carbon fiber. Boeing also wanted to build it in a new way.

Instead of building most of the plane itself, it spread the work across more than fifty partners worldwide. By Boeing's own figure, partners would supply sixty-five percent of the airframe. [B13] Whole sections would arrive ready-made, and Boeing would snap them together.

> Énoncé : « Less money up front. More risk later. »

The idea was to put in less cash up front and share the risk. Instead, parts arrived late or unfinished, and Boeing had to fix them.

> Chronologie : 2004 lancement ; 2011 première livraison ; 2013 batteries ; 2021-2022 livraisons suspendues.

The first 787 reached a customer, All Nippon Airways, in 2011, about three years late. One estimate put the program twenty-five billion dollars over budget. [B14] In January 2013, battery fires grounded the whole fleet for about three months. [B14]

And it didn't end there. From 2021 to August 2022, Boeing stopped delivering 787s over manufacturing defects, like tiny gaps in the fuselage. About a hundred and twenty finished Dreamliners piled up, waiting. [B15]

> Barres : 2013-2019, rachats d'actions 43,4 Md$ contre R&D avions commerciaux 15,7 Md$.

Meanwhile, money was going somewhere else. From 2013 to 2019, Boeing spent forty-three billion dollars buying back its own shares, according to a Fortune analysis. Over the same six years, it spent less than sixteen billion on research and development for its commercial airplanes. [B9] In 2016, it promised to hand back all of its free cash flow to shareholders. [B9]

Buybacks are legal and common. But that money isn't spent on the next airplane. And Boeing needed one.

## 4. The MAX

> Photo : Airbus A320neo. Énoncé : « 2010 : Airbus puts new engines on the A320. »

Its best-selling plane, the 737, dated back to the 1960s. In 2010, Airbus put new, fuel-saving engines on its rival, the A320neo, and airlines rushed to order it.

> Silhouette : l'avion vu de dessus, le moteur qui avance sous l'aile.

Boeing could design a brand-new airplane, which takes many years and many billions. Or put new engines on the old 737, faster and much cheaper. In 2011, it chose the second. One analyst later said, "Their hand was forced." [B16] That plane was the 737 MAX.

> Schéma simple : capteur d'angle d'attaque, une seule flèche vers « MCAS ».

The new engines were bigger, so they sat further forward, and changed how the plane handled in some situations. Boeing added software called MCAS to make it feel like the older 737. And it could be triggered by a single sensor. [B17]

> Énoncé : « Not in the pilots' manuals. »

A congressional report later accused Boeing of "concealing the very existence of MCAS from 737 MAX pilots". [B17]

> Écran sobre, sans image : « Lion Air 610, October 2018 : 189 people ». Puis « Ethiopian Airlines 302, March 2019 : 157 people ».

In October 2018, a Lion Air 737 MAX crashed in Indonesia. All one hundred and eighty-nine people on board died. Five months later, in March 2019, an Ethiopian Airlines MAX crashed near Addis Ababa. One hundred and fifty-seven people died. [B18]

> Énoncé, fond noir : « 346 ».

Three hundred and forty-six people in total.

> Photo : MAX stationnés. Chronologie : mars 2019, immobilisation ; novembre 2020, retour.

Regulators grounded the MAX in March 2019. It stayed grounded for twenty months, until November 2020, after Boeing changed the software to read two sensors instead of one. [B17] [B19]

> Citation du rapport de la Chambre, 2020.

In 2020, a US House committee called the crashes the "horrific culmination" of "faulty design and performance assumptions", "a lack of transparency" by Boeing's management, and failed oversight by the regulator, the FAA. [B17]

> Reçu : ce que l'immobilisation a coûté à Boeing.

By one count, the grounding cost Boeing about twenty billion dollars in direct costs. [B20] In 2021, the Justice Department charged Boeing with fraud over the plane's certification. In November 2025, a judge dropped the case at the department's request, after Boeing agreed to pay over a billion dollars more, including for the victims' families. The judge wrote that the deal "fails to secure the necessary accountability", but that he had no power to stop it. [B21]

The MAX was flying again. Then came January 2024.

## 5. The door plug

> Photo : 737 MAX 9 d'Alaska Airlines. Silhouette : l'emplacement du bouchon de porte, à l'arrière gauche.

On January fifth, 2024, Alaska Airlines Flight twelve eighty-two was climbing out of Portland, Oregon, at about fourteen thousand eight hundred feet, when a panel blew out of the side of the cabin. [B22]

The panel was a door plug, covering an extra emergency exit this airline didn't use. The cabin lost pressure in seconds. The crew turned back and landed in Portland. No one was killed. Seven passengers and one flight attendant had minor injuries. [B22]

> Gros chiffre : « 4 bolts ».

The plane was almost new. Investigators found that the four bolts meant to hold the plug in place were missing. [B23] The plug was installed by Spirit AeroSystems, Boeing's fuselage supplier in Wichita, Kansas. At Boeing's factory in Renton, near Seattle, it was opened to repair rivets, and photos show it was closed again without the bolts. [B23]

> Photo : usine de Renton. Énoncé : « No paperwork. »

Boeing said there was no record of the work. [B23] Its chief executive, Dave Calhoun, said: "Whatever final conclusions are reached, Boeing is accountable for what happened." [B23]

> Logo FAA. Tuiles : 171 avions immobilisés, plafond à 38 par mois, plan sous 90 jours.

The next day, the FAA grounded a hundred and seventy-one 737 MAX 9s. [B24] It capped MAX production at thirty-eight a month, [B25] and its audit found Boeing hadn't complied with quality rules on how it controlled its processes and handled parts. The FAA's chief said Boeing "must commit to real and profound improvements". [B24]

> Logo NTSB. Citation de Jennifer Homendy, 2025.

In June 2025, the safety board, the NTSB, blamed Boeing's failure to follow its own manufacturing procedures, and weak oversight by the FAA. Its chair, Jennifer Homendy, said: "the crew shouldn't have had to be heroes, because this accident never should have happened." [B26]

> Énoncé : « Spirit AeroSystems : sold in 2005, bought back in 2025. »

One more twist. Spirit's Wichita plant used to be part of Boeing. Boeing sold it in 2005, and bought it back in December 2025. [B33]

## 6. The strike

> Photo : grève de 2024. Compteur de jours : 53.

By March 2024, Calhoun had announced he would step down. [B27] In August, Boeing brought in an outsider, Kelly Ortberg, an engineer by training. [B28] He had barely arrived when the factories stopped.

On September thirteenth, 2024, thirty-three thousand machinists walked out, in Seattle and Portland. They wanted big raises, and their old pension back, frozen about a decade earlier. The strike lasted fifty-three days. [B29]

> Tuiles : +38 % sur quatre ans, 12 000 $ de prime, retraite non rétablie.

They went back in November with a thirty-eight percent raise over four years, but not the pension. [B29] Bank of America estimated the strike cost Boeing about fifty million dollars a day. [B29]

> Reçu : la facture de 2024.

Ortberg moved fast. He announced about seventeen thousand job cuts, [B29] and sold up to twenty-four billion dollars of new shares, to protect Boeing's credit rating. [B30] Five years earlier, Boeing was buying back its stock. Now it was selling it.

> Barres : flux de trésorerie disponible, 2024 : −14,3 Md$.

For 2024, Boeing lost almost twelve billion dollars, and burned more than fourteen billion in cash. [B4]

## 7. Where Boeing stands now

> Photo : Boeing Field ou 737 neufs. Compteur : 600 livraisons en 2025.

So where is Boeing now? In 2025, it delivered six hundred airplanes, its most since 2018. [B4] Airbus delivered seven hundred and ninety-three. [B31] But for the first time in seven years, Boeing won more new orders than Airbus. [B31]

> Jauge : cadence du 737, aiguille 38, puis 42, puis 47 par mois.

The FAA let it raise 737 production to forty-two a month in October 2025, [B32] and Boeing says it's now moving toward forty-seven. [B5]

It even reported a profit for 2025, about two point two billion dollars, but only thanks to a nine point six billion dollar gain from selling a business. [B4]

> Tuiles : résultats du T3 2026.

The latest numbers are from July to September 2026. Revenue: twenty-four point two billion dollars. Net loss: a hundred and sixteen million, down from more than five billion a year earlier. And it generated almost half a billion dollars of free cash. [B5]

> Barres : marge par division : services +18,3 %, défense +6,2 %, avions commerciaux −6,5 %.

But look inside. Boeing's services business made about eighteen cents of profit on every dollar. The division that builds airliners still lost about six and a half cents on every dollar. [B5]

> Tuiles : dette 45,9 Md$, trésorerie 20,7 Md$, carnet 745 Md$.

Boeing owes about forty-six billion dollars, against about twenty-one billion in cash. And it has orders worth seven hundred and forty-five billion dollars waiting, including more than six thousand three hundred airplanes. [B5]

> Chronologie : 737-10 certifié en octobre 2026, 777X attendu en 2027.

The smallest and largest versions of the MAX are now certified. [B5] The new 777X, after a four point nine billion dollar charge, should reach its first customer in 2027, years late. [B34] [B5]

As Ortberg put it in October: "But we are not done." [B5]

## 8. The real cost

> Grand récapitulatif : chronologie entière 1954-2026, les factures qui s'allument.

So what really went wrong at Boeing? Not one decision, but a long series of them, each saving money or time at first: a merger that brought in a finance-first culture, a Dreamliner built by outsiders, an old jet with new engines, and billions handed back to shareholders.

> Énoncé : « Saved early. Paid later. »

And each time, the bill came later, and bigger. Twenty billion for the grounding. Three years of delays on the Dreamliner. Almost thirty-six billion in losses in six years. And a cost no number can measure: three hundred and forty-six lives.

> Énoncé : « In aviation, the engineering is the business. »

In most industries, cutting costs makes a product cheaper. In aviation, cutting corners doesn't remove the cost. It moves it into the future, where it's much bigger.

Boeing is now trying to pay that bill down, one airplane at a time. Whether it can, we'll know from the factory floor, not from the next quarter.
