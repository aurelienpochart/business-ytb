## 0. Hook

> Logo Costco (usage informatif). Un ticket de caisse géant se déroule ; en bas, deux lignes en Plex Mono : « Merchandise: $297B » et « Membership: $5.9B ».

In its last financial year, Costco sold two hundred and ninety-seven billion dollars' worth of stuff. Groceries, TVs, tires, gasoline, and a very large number of rotisserie chickens. [R1]

After paying for all those goods, and for every warehouse, every worker and every truck, about five point eight billion dollars were left from those sales. Less than two cents on the dollar. [R2]

Then there's a second line in the accounts. Five point nine billion dollars, from eighty-four million people paying for a plastic card. [R1] [R4]

Those fees are about two percent of Costco's revenue. And about half of its operating profit. [R2]

So here's the claim we're going to test. Costco isn't really a store that charges a fee. It's a membership that comes with a store attached.

Let's take the receipt apart, line by line.

> Ident The Cost Curve, 3 s.

## 1. The receipt: one hundred dollars at the register

> Un billet de 100 $ se découpe en tranches, en cascade : 88,91 $ (gris), 9,15 $ (bleu-vert), 1,94 $ (ambre), puis une tranche ambre de 1,99 $ « membership » qui s'ajoute à côté.

Costco's financial year ended on August thirtieth, 2026. Total revenue: three hundred and three billion dollars. Almost all of it is what Costco calls net sales, the goods. The rest, five point nine billion, is membership fees. [R1]

Now follow one hundred dollars spent at the checkout.

About eighty-nine dollars go straight back to the companies that made the goods. That's what Costco paid for the merchandise. [R2]

Another nine dollars and fifteen cents pay for running everything else: wages, warehouses, electricity, card fees, head office. [R2]

What's left from the sale itself: one dollar and ninety-four cents. [R2]

Now add the membership. For every hundred dollars of sales, members also pay Costco about two dollars in fees. [R2] And unlike a sale, a fee doesn't come with any goods to pay for.

Put the two together, and Costco makes about three dollars and ninety-three cents of operating profit on every hundred dollars of sales. After interest and taxes, it keeps about three dollars and ten cents. [R2]

> Barre unique « Operating income $11.7B » coupée en deux moitiés : « from selling goods » et « from membership fees ».

Over the whole year, that's eleven point seven billion dollars of operating profit. [R1] Roughly half came from what's on the shelves. The other half came from the card. [R2]

For comparison, American food retailers keep about two cents of net profit per dollar, according to their industry group. [R7] So Costco isn't poor. It just makes its money in a strange place.

Which raises the question: why would a store choose to make so little on the things it sells?

## 2. The shelves: priced to barely break even

> Courbe plate : marge brute de Costco à 11,09 %, puis une ligne en pointillés « typical supermarket » nettement plus haut (sans chiffre, ou chiffre sourcé si ajouté).

On everything it sells, Costco's gross margin was eleven point oh nine percent last year. [R2] That's the slice left after paying suppliers, before any other cost. Costco's own annual report says its volume lets it operate profitably at "significantly lower gross margins" than most retailers. [R6]

That's not an accident. For decades, Costco has been reported to cap its markups: around fourteen percent on national brands, and fifteen percent on its own brand. [R8] It's not written in the annual report. But the eleven percent is.

> Deux produits côte à côte, étiquettes de prix animées : noix Kirkland 13,79 $ → 9,99 $ ; poivre noir 6,99 $ → 5,99 $.

That own brand is Kirkland Signature. Costco's finance chief says Kirkland products are priced at least fifteen to twenty percent below the national brand equivalent. [R9] Store brands made up just under a third of Costco's sales in late 2024. [R9]

And when Costco's own costs fall, it tends to pass the saving on. On its latest earnings call, it listed price cuts: Kirkland walnuts from thirteen seventy-nine to nine ninety-nine. Black pepper from six ninety-nine to five ninety-nine. [R9]

> Photo réelle du stand de restauration (licence à vérifier) ou illustration simple : un hot-dog et un gobelet, étiquette « $1.50 since 1985 », puis un compteur d'inflation qui monte à « $4.65 ».

Then there's the most famous price in retail. A hot dog and a soda for one dollar and fifty cents. Same price since 1985. [R10] If it had followed inflation, it would cost about four sixty-five today. [R10]

In March 2026, Costco's chief executive, Ron Vachris, was asked about it. His answer: "The hot dog price will not change as long as I'm around." [R10]

Why would a company with three hundred billion dollars of sales care about a hot dog? Because at Costco, a price isn't only a way to make money on an item. It's an advertisement for the card. Every member who sees a one-fifty hot dog, or a cheaper bag of walnuts, gets one more reason to renew.

A normal retailer that finds a cheaper supplier keeps the difference as profit. Costco mostly passes it on. In its finance chief's words: "Our goal is to be the first to lower prices where we see opportunities to do so." [R9] A member who feels the savings is worth more than a few extra cents on one item.

But selling at eleven percent margins only works if you can also run a store for less than anyone else.

## 3. How to run a store on eleven cents

> Comparaison à l'échelle : un supermarché moyen (33 248 références, 42 000 pieds carrés) et un entrepôt Costco (moins de 4 000 références, 148 000 pieds carrés). Les références sont des petits carrés : l'un en est couvert, l'autre presque vide.

Here's the first trick. Choice.

An average American supermarket carries about thirty-three thousand different items. [R7] A Costco warehouse is more than three times bigger, and carries fewer than four thousand. [R6] [R7]

Think one or two brands of ketchup, not twenty. In a giant pack. On a pallet.

That does a lot of work. With so few items, each one sells in enormous volume. Costco's annual report says it plainly: those volumes, and how fast the goods turn over, are what let it live on such thin margins. [R6] Fewer items also means bigger orders for each supplier, and fewer, bigger things to unload and stock.

> Barres : ventes par bâtiment, Costco ≈ 317 M$ contre Sam's Club ≈ 155 M$.

The result is a staggering amount of sales per building. Costco runs nine hundred and thirty-nine warehouses. Divide its sales by that, and each one does more than three hundred million dollars a year. [R1] [R16] Its closest rival, Walmart's Sam's Club, runs six hundred and one clubs, with ninety-three billion dollars of sales. That's about one hundred and fifty-five million each. Roughly half. [R15] [R16]

Here's the part that surprises people. Costco isn't cheap because it pays people badly.

It has about three hundred and fifty-five thousand employees. In the US, the average hourly wage is around thirty-three dollars. [R11] In early 2025, it set its lowest starting rate at twenty dollars an hour. [R11]

And yet all its running costs, wages included, come to just nine point one five percent of sales. [R2] Not because each worker is cheap, but because each worker moves so much merchandise.

So the shelves make a little money, in a very efficient way. Now for the line that makes the rest.

## 4. The card: what members are really buying

> Compteurs : 84,1 M de membres payants, 150,4 M de porteurs de carte, 42,3 M d'Executive. Puis un anneau : 92,3 % de renouvellement (États-Unis et Canada).

At the end of August 2026, Costco had eighty-four point one million paid members. With the extra household cards, that's one hundred and fifty million people carrying one. [R4]

In the US, a basic membership costs sixty-five dollars a year. [R5] About half of paid members pay double that for the Executive card, one hundred and thirty dollars, which gives back two percent on most purchases, up to twelve hundred and fifty dollars a year. [R4] [R5]

Now the number that matters most. In the US and Canada, ninety-two point three percent of members renew. [R4]

Think about what that means. Almost every year, more than nine in ten customers pay again, up front, before they've bought anything. Costco can count that money in advance, and it spreads it evenly over the year in its accounts. [R4]

Costco says it openly. In its latest earnings call, Ron Vachris called membership "the most important item we sell". [R4]

> Ligne de temps : 2017, dernière hausse ; 1er septembre 2024 : 60 $ → 65 $, 120 $ → 130 $ ; puis barres des cotisations FY2025 → FY2026 (+11 %).

And like any product, it can get a price increase. In September 2024, Costco raised its fees for the first time since 2017: sixty to sixty-five dollars for the basic card, one hundred and twenty to one hundred and thirty for Executive. [R5]

Over the past year, membership income grew eleven percent. [R2] And renewal didn't fall. It ticked up. [R4]

The benefit from that increase is now fully in the numbers. In the last quarter, leaving aside the increase and currency effects, fee income still grew almost seven percent, simply because there are more members, and more of them on the expensive card. [R5] [R4]

And the members are getting younger. According to Ron Vachris, the number of members under forty has grown nearly sixty percent since the pandemic. They're now more than a quarter of the base. [R4] A thirty-year-old who renews today could keep paying for decades.

Here's the deal, then, in one sentence. Members pay Costco for the right to buy things at almost cost. And Costco has to keep those prices low, every single day, or that ninety-two percent starts to slip.

That's the real discipline behind the hot dog. Raise prices on the shelves, and you put the membership at risk. And the membership is where half the profit is.

## 5. The extras: gas, the app, and the rivals

> Carte des États-Unis : 774 stations-service qui s'allument. Puis une flèche « pump → warehouse ».

Two parts of Costco are growing faster than the rest, and both serve the card.

First, gasoline. Costco ran seven hundred and seventy-four gas stations at the end of its year, and fuel made up eleven percent of its sales. [R12] When prices at the pump jumped in 2026, drivers came in droves: in the last quarter, gas sales were up by more than thirty percent. [R12]

Costco doesn't say how much it earns on fuel. But it says gas brings in new members, and that members who fill up there tend to spend more inside. [R12]

Second, online. Costco's digital sales passed thirty-three billion dollars last year, up more than twenty percent. [R13] Same model, just without the walk past the pallets.

> Barres mensuelles : ventes de septembre 2026, 30 Md$, +13 %.

And the machine is still running hot. In September 2026, Costco's sales were up thirteen percent. Without the effect of gas prices and currencies, comparable sales were up almost eight percent. [R14]

> Logos (usage informatif) : Sam's Club, Walmart, Amazon, BJ's.

What about the competition? In its annual report, Costco lists Walmart, Target, Kroger and Amazon, plus the other warehouse clubs, Sam's Club and BJ's. [R15]

Several of them run the same playbook. Walmart says membership income is a significant part of Sam's Club's profit. [R15] And Amazon Prime is another way of charging shoppers up front, so that they come back.

Costco's answer is to keep building. It opened twenty-eight warehouses last year, and plans thirty-three more this year, including in Europe, Canada and Mexico. [R6] Every new building is a new pool of people who can buy a card.

So the real fight isn't over who sells the cheapest ketchup. It's over which membership people decide is worth paying for again next year.

## 6. Conclusion

> Retour au billet de 100 $ : la tranche « membership » pulse en ambre ; à côté, le hot-dog à 1,50 $ et la carte de membre.

So, how does Costco make money?

On the goods, barely. About two dollars on every hundred, after costs. [R2] On the card, a lot. Almost six billion dollars a year, for a product that doesn't need a shelf. [R1]

That doesn't mean the shelves don't matter. Without the cheap walnuts, the pallets and the one-fifty hot dog, nobody would pay sixty-five dollars to walk in. The shelves are the proof. The card is the business.

And the lesson goes beyond Costco. The cheapest thing in the building is often there for a reason. Sometimes the price on the shelf isn't how a company makes money. It's how it convinces you to pay somewhere else.
